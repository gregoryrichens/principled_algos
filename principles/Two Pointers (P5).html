Two Pointers

Put a finger at each end of a sorted list. One comparison tells you that one end can never be part of the answer. Remove that end and move in.

Principle 5 (P5) of 18. Elsewhere, P1 means "keep a dictionary of numbers you have seen" and P8 means "sliding window".

As if to a child

You have exactly 10 coins. You must buy exactly two toys and spend all 10 coins. The toys are on a shelf in order of price, from cheapest on the left to most expensive on the right: 1, 3, 4, 6, 8, 11.

Put your left hand on the cheapest toy and your right hand on the most expensive toy. Add the two prices. 1 + 11 = 12. That is too much. Now think about the 11-coin toy. Even with the cheapest toy on the shelf, it costs too much. So the 11-coin toy cannot be in the answer. Put it back and move your right hand one toy to the left. If the total is too small, do the opposite: the cheap toy is too cheap even with the most expensive partner, so move your left hand to the right. Your hands walk toward each other. They stop when the total is exactly 10, or when the hands meet. If they meet, no two toys cost exactly 10.

The extended explanation

Start from what is true

Start with three facts that we can be sure of.

  • Fact 1. The list is sorted. Every number to the right of a position is the same or bigger. Every number to the left is the same or smaller.
  • Fact 2. A sum gets bigger when one of its parts gets bigger. If a ≤ b, then a + x ≤ b + x.
  • Fact 3. The slow way tries every pair. With n numbers there are n × (n − 1) ÷ 2 pairs. For 6 numbers that is 15. For large n it is about n × n ÷ 2. This is the cost we want to cut.

Now join Fact 1 and Fact 2. The left pointer is at position l and the right pointer is at position r. All the numbers still in play are between them.

  • If numbers[l] + numbers[r] is too big: numbers[l] is the smallest partner that numbers[r] can still get. Every other partner gives the same sum or a bigger one. So numbers[r] is in no answer.
  • If the sum is too small: numbers[r] is the biggest partner that numbers[l] can still get. Every other partner gives the same sum or a smaller one. So numbers[l] is in no answer.

One test removes not one pair but a whole group of pairs. That is the full idea.

Look at the result. The method tested 5 squares. Four tests each removed a full row or column. The fifth test found the answer. Together they settled all 15 pairs. Each removed row or column has a one-line proof, and that proof is the algorithm.

The sentence that keeps the code correct

Any pair that uses a number outside [l, r] is already ruled out.

This sentence is the invariant: a statement that stays true after every step. At the start it is true, because nothing is outside the range. Each move keeps it true, because we only remove a number when we have a proof. When the pointers meet, nothing is left to test. Use it as a test for new problems: if you cannot say why the dropped number is hopeless, two pointers is probably the wrong tool.

Napkin math: is it really that much faster?

Do the math once by hand for one million numbers. Pairs: 106 × 106 ÷ 2 = 5 × 1011. At 109 per second, that is 500 seconds, or about 8 minutes. Two pointers: at most 106 steps, which is 0.001 seconds. The ratio is 5 × 1011 ÷ 106 = 500,000. The claim "a million times faster" is exaggerated by about two times. The correct value is about half a million.

Why not use a dictionary, as in P1? It also takes about n steps. But it must store up to n numbers. Two pointers stores two positions. Interview problems call this "O(1) extra memory": a fixed amount of memory that does not grow with the list. When a problem gives a sorted list and asks for O(1) extra memory, try two pointers first.

Worked example 1: Two Sum II (LeetCode 167)

Problem. The list is sorted. Return the positions (counted from 1) of the two numbers that add to the target. Use no extra list or dictionary. The problem promises that exactly one answer exists.

def twoSum(numbers, target):
    l, r = 0, len(numbers) - 1
    while l < r:
        total = numbers[l] + numbers[r]
        if total == target:
            return [l + 1, r + 1]   # positions count from 1
        if total > target:
            r -= 1                  # numbers[r] is too big for every partner left
        else:
            l += 1                  # numbers[l] is too small for every partner left
    return []                       # pointers met: no pair (cannot happen in 167)

Worked example 2: Container With Most Water (LeetCode 11)

Problem. Each number is the height of a vertical wall. Pick two walls. The water they hold is shorter wall × distance between them. Find the most water.

This list is not sorted. So what lets us drop an end? Build the proof from scratch. Take the two walls under the pointers. The water level is set by the shorter wall. Any partner for that wall that is still in play sits between the pointers, so it is closer. (Partners outside the pointers are already ruled out by the invariant.) A closer partner makes a narrower container. Its level cannot be higher than the shorter wall. Narrower and not taller means less water. So the shorter wall has already done its best. Drop it. If the two walls have the same height, the same argument works for each of them, so drop either one. The proof differs from Two Sum II, but its shape is the same.

Blue fill: water for the pair under test. Dashed box: best so far. Each step has two clicks: measure, then drop. When the pointers meet, the status line checks the answer against the slow way (all pairs). Try random walls. The answers always agree.

def maxArea(height):
    l, r = 0, len(height) - 1
    best = 0
    while l < r:
        best = max(best, min(height[l], height[r]) * (r - l))
        if height[l] < height[r]:
            l += 1     # the left wall cannot do better with a closer partner
        else:
            r -= 1     # the right wall is shorter or equal: same reason
    return best

On [1, 8, 6, 2, 5, 4, 8, 3, 7] the answer is 49: walls of height 8 and 7, seven positions apart, so 7 × 7 = 49.

Worked example 3: 3Sum (LeetCode 15)

Problem. Return every different group of three numbers that adds to 0. No group can appear twice.

Turn the new problem into the old one. Sort the list, then fix the first number with a normal loop. For each first number a, you need two more numbers that add to −a. That is Two Sum II on the rest of the list, with two changes. First, there can be many answers, so after a match the search continues. Second, sorting puts equal numbers side by side, so you skip a number that is the same as the one before it. Do this for the first number and for the second number. Without the second skip, [-2, 0, 0, 2, 2] records [-2, 0, 2] twice.

def threeSum(nums):
    nums.sort()
    res = []
    for i in range(len(nums)):
        if i > 0 and nums[i] == nums[i - 1]:
            continue                     # same first number: same groups
        l, r = i + 1, len(nums) - 1      # Two Sum II for target -nums[i]
        while l < r:
            total = nums[i] + nums[l] + nums[r]
            if total < 0:
                l += 1
            elif total > 0:
                r -= 1
            else:
                res.append([nums[i], nums[l], nums[r]])
                l += 1
                r -= 1                   # keep searching for more groups
                while l < r and nums[l] == nums[l - 1]:
                    l += 1               # same second number: same group
    return res

Cost for 1,000 numbers. A good sort takes about n × log₂ n steps, where log₂ n is how many times you can halve n to reach 1. For 1,000 that is about 1,000 × 10 = 10,000 steps. Then 1,000 first numbers, each with a search of up to 1,000 steps, give about 1,000,000 steps. The slow way tests every group of three. There are 1,000 × 999 × 998 ÷ 6 ≈ 166,000,000 groups. We divide by 6 because the same three numbers can be put in 6 orders. Two pointers does about 160 times less work.

The same idea, other shapes

The core of P5 is two pointers that move toward each other. A test on the two ends tells you which end to drop. The same "one test, many pairs removed" idea also works in these shapes:

ShapeExampleWhat one test proves
Move inwardValid Palindrome (125)The outer two letters match, so they cannot break the palindrome. Drop both and check the inner part.
Move outwardLongest Palindromic Substring (5)Start at each center and grow while the ends match. At the first mismatch, every longer string with this center fails too. Stop.
Same direction, two listsMerge Two Sorted Lists (21)The smaller front item is smaller than every item not yet taken, so it goes next.

Do not mix this up with a sliding window (P8). Both use two positions. In P5, you care only about the two items under the pointers. In a sliding window, both pointers move the same way along one list, and you care about every item between them, for example their sum.

Harder: why does Two Sum (LeetCode 1) need a dictionary instead?

Two Sum gives an unsorted list, so Fact 1 is false. When 1 + 11 is too big, a smaller number in the middle can still work with 11. You cannot prove that 11 is hopeless, so you cannot drop it. P1 solves it instead: walk the list once, and for each number check a dictionary for the partner you need.

In the real world

A story from 1945. In The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 3, Donald Knuth reports that John von Neumann wrote one of the first programs for a stored-program computer in 1945. It was a merge: take two sorted stacks of records and make one sorted stack. One pointer sits at the front of each stack. Compare the two front items and take the smaller. This is the same-direction shape from the table.

  • Search engines. For each word, the index keeps a sorted list of the pages that contain it. To find pages with both "red" and "boat", walk one pointer down each list. Move the pointer at the smaller page number. When both pointers show the same number, that page has both words.
  • Databases. A "merge join" matches rows from two tables that are already sorted by the same key, such as a customer number. It uses the same walk. The other common method, a hash join, first builds a dictionary of one whole table in memory. When the sort order already exists, the merge join does not need that dictionary.

In each case, a sorted order lets one comparison make a safe decision about many items. That saves time and memory.
